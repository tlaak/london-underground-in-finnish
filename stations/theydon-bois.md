# Theydon Bois

Theydon Bois is served by the Central line. It is a village on the edge of Epping Forest, in the Epping Forest district of Essex, north-east of London — famous locally for having no street lights.

## Summary

| London Underground station | Finnish translation |
| -------------------------- | ------------------- |
| Theydon Bois               | Ruokolaakso-Metsälä |

## Background

The name is a compound of two unrelated historical layers, which is what makes it so hard to translate.

**Theydon** comes from Old English _þæc + denu_ and was [first recorded as _Thecdene_ in 1062](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theydon_Bois): "the valley where thatching material is gathered." _þæc_ is literally "thatch," and _denu_ is a valley.

**Bois** was added later, from the Norman [de Bois family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theydon_Bois) (also spelled _de Boys_ or _de Bosco_), who held the manor in the 12th and 13th centuries. It is one of three neighbouring Theydons distinguished by the families that held them — the others being Theydon Garnon and Theydon Mount. The de Bois surname happens to mean "wood" in French, and the spelling was deliberately kept because of the village's position on the edge of Epping Forest. "Bois" therefore carries a triple resonance at once: a family name, the literal meaning _wood_, and the real forest next door. (Despite the French look, it is pronounced locally as "Boyce" or "Boys.")

## Direct translations

| English                | Finnish        |
| ---------------------- | -------------- |
| thatch (_þæc_)         | ruoko, olki    |
| valley (_denu_)        | laakso, notko  |
| wood, de Bois (_bois_) | metsä, Metsälä |

For **Theydon**, the thatching material is the key. Traditional Finnish thatched roofs (_ruokokatto_) are made of _ruoko_ — reed — which is precisely the plant one gathers from a wet valley floor, exactly as the Old English name describes. _Olki_ (straw) is the other candidate, but straw is a by-product of grain fields, not something "got from a valley," so _ruoko_ is the truer fit. Combined with _laakso_ (valley) this gives _Ruokolaakso_, "reed valley" — and it stands on real Finnish ground, echoing the municipality of [Ruokolahti](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruokolahti) and the place-and-surname _Ruokola_.

For **Bois**, the elegant move is to reproduce in Finnish the very same wordplay the English name relies on. _Bois_ is a surname that also means "wood"; the natural Finnish counterpart is the real surname and place name _Metsälä_ — literally "the wood-place." Rendering Bois as _Metsälä_ keeps all three of its English resonances: it reads as a manorial family name, it means "wood," and it points to the neighbouring forest.

## Conclusion

Theydon Bois is built as a base settlement name plus a distinguishing manorial second element, just as _Itä-Tammela_ and _Länsi-Tammela_ pair a core name with a distinguishing prefix. The Finnish translation keeps that two-part shape, hyphenated in the same spirit: _Ruokolaakso_ ("reed/thatch valley") for Theydon, and _Metsälä_ ("wood-place," the Wood family's holding) for Bois.

The name could have been trimmed to a lean _Ruokolaakso_, dropping Bois as a mere tag. But that would throw away the better half of the joke — the coincidence, preserved on purpose by the villagers themselves, that the manorial family's name also means "wood" and the wood is really there. Finnish can land the same wink through _Metsälä_, so it is worth keeping. Therefore Theydon Bois in Finnish shall be called _Ruokolaakso-Metsälä_, with the plainer _Ruokolaakso_ noted as the shorter alternative.
